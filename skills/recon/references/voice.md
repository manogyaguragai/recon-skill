# Voice

The failure mode this file exists to prevent: a draft that reads as machine
written. Faculty now screen for it explicitly, and some publish warnings that
generic AI-generated mail does more harm than sending nothing. A bad draft does
not fail neutrally - it burns the contact permanently, and the user never finds
out it happened.

## The core insight

The thing that makes writing read as human and the thing that makes a cold
email convert are the same thing: **specific, unusual, hard-to-fabricate
detail.** Models round specifics off. People hoard them.

This means the provenance-marked inventory is not only an honesty mechanism, it
is the authenticity mechanism. Verified specifics are the raw material. A draft
built from `document` and `verified_link` facts about a real person's real work
is hard to write and hard to fake, which is exactly why it lands.

It also means the fix for a weak draft is never "add polish". It is "find a
more specific true thing".

## Voice calibration

Before drafting, read the user's `writing_sample` and note:

- sentence length patterns - short and punchy, long and flowing, or mixed
- word choice level - casual, academic, in between
- how they open - straight in, or context first
- punctuation habits - dashes, parentheticals, semicolons
- recurring phrases and verbal tics
- transitions - explicit connectors, or just the next point

Then replace AI patterns with patterns **from the sample**, not with generic
alternatives. If they write short sentences, do not produce long ones. If they
say "stuff" and "things", do not upgrade to "elements" and "components".

Without a sample, ask for one. Any 200 words they actually wrote - an old
statement of purpose, a project README, a long message. This is the single
highest-leverage input to the whole stage and it takes the user two minutes.

## Register

A cold academic email is formal register. Neutral and plain **is** the correct
human voice here. Do not inject opinions, jokes, first-person colour, or
deliberate mess - that produces the corny, trying-too-hard tone that gets
deleted. The goal is a competent person writing plainly, not a personality.

Industry, recruiter, and employer-funder contacts are a different register and
tolerate more warmth and more visual material.

## Structure

Short. Four moves:

1. **The hook** - one or two lines on their work, specific enough to prove it
   was read. Best material is a limitation or future-work thread from a paper
   published in the last one to three years
2. **The bridge** - the actual connection between their problem and the user's
   capability. This is the load-bearing sentence and the one most drafts skip
3. **The user** - short, concrete, verifiable
4. **The ask** - clear, small, easy to say yes to

Attach a polished CV. Plain text for academics; richer assets only for industry
contacts. An infographic or a video link to a professor reads as marketing, and
marketing is the smell being filtered for.

If the contact has published a required subject line or a preferred process,
follow it exactly.

## Funding is a lever, and where it is a strong one

In systems where the supervisor pays from a project budget, an applicant with
their own funding removes the single biggest practical barrier to being hired.
Where the user has secured or is applying for portable funding, say so plainly
and early. Where they have not, do not lead with the ask.

Never open by asking for money.

## The audit loop

Do not ship the first draft. Run this every time:

1. Write the draft
2. Ask explicitly: **"What makes this read as AI generated?"** Answer honestly
   in a few bullets. This forced self-critique catches more than any checklist
3. Revise against your own answer
4. Run the find-and-replace test: swap the professor's name and institution for
   another. If the email still works, the hook is generic. Discard and find a
   real one
5. Run the provenance test: every claim about the user traces to `document` or
   `verified_link`. Anything `stated` gets confirmed with the user or cut

If step 4 cannot be passed - if there is no specific true hook - **say so
instead of shipping filler.** Recommend dropping the target or doing more
reading. A weak email is worse than no email.

## Patterns to strip

Inflated significance ("stands as a testament", "pivotal", "underscores the
importance", "evolving landscape"). Promotional adjectives. Rule-of-three
cadence. Negative parallelism ("not just X, but Y"). Filler openers ("I hope
this email finds you well" is tolerated but adds nothing). Superficial -ing
clauses tacked onto sentence ends. Vague attribution ("experts say", "it is
widely recognised"). Manufactured-candour openers ("Honestly? It depends").
Em dashes in the final version.

## What not to strip

Do not over-correct. These are not tells and gutting them damages the draft:
perfect grammar, formal vocabulary, a salutation and sign-off, one "however",
a single short emphatic sentence, curly quotes. Look for clusters, not isolated
patterns. A user's genuinely formal prose should stay formal - flattening it
into fake casualness is its own kind of fake.

## Replies

A reply is the highest-leverage moment in the process and the one most often
fumbled - too eager, too long, or three days late.

When a reply arrives: flag it, update status, and offer to draft a response.
Keep the loop open for that contact until it resolves. Match their length and
register; a two-line question gets a short answer, not an essay. Answer what
was asked before adding anything else.
